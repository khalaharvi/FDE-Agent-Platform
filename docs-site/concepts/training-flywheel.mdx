The human gates are not just a safety mechanism — they are a labeling
operation that nobody has to staff. Reviewers doing their normal job produce
high-quality training signal as a side effect:

| Gate outcome | Training signal |
|---|---|
| Merged, no edits | Positive — the trajectory and its output were correct |
| Rejected | Negative |
| **Edited** | **Paired preference** — original versus corrected payload, same input |
| Expired | Discarded — the absence of a decision is not a decision |
| Approved in seconds | Discarded — a rubber stamp is not a label |

The edited case is the valuable one. A reviewer who rewords a proposal before
approving it has produced a (rejected, chosen) pair on identical input —
exactly the shape preference optimization wants, for free. This is why the
review console is designed to make editing as easy as approving: the reviewer
edit is the most valuable event in the system.

The discards matter as much as the labels. Training on rubber stamps means
training on noise that is correlated with reviewer fatigue — worse than
random noise, because it is systematic.

None of this works unless the review decision is genuinely human. That is one
of the three reasons the gates are [deterministic SQL](/concepts/human-gates):
if an LLM decided which proposals needed review, the training signal would be
partly the model grading itself.

## Why traces are trainable

Every agent invocation records a trace with two load-bearing fields:

- **The pinned graph commit** the agent could see. Without it a trace is
  untrainable — you cannot reconstruct what was retrievable, so you cannot
  tell whether a miss was the policy's fault or the graph's.
- **A per-turn trainable flag** — false on every tool-result and user turn.
  This is the loss mask, computed once at capture rather than re-derived (and
  mis-derived) by each downstream consumer.

The mask is the highest-stakes detail in the pipeline. Train on retrieved
tokens and you either add gradient noise from content the policy does not
control, or — worse — teach the model to *generate* plausible-looking
retrieval results instead of calling the tool. You would be training a
hallucinator on retrieval data. The export tooling validates the mask and
fails loudly rather than warning, because a silent mask bug costs a full
training run and you find out at eval.

## Most teams should stop before RL

The pipeline is staged, and each stage has an explicit gate to proceed:

1. **Prompt and context engineering first.** Retrieval tuning, prompt
   structure, tool design. This is where the largest wins usually are, and it
   costs zero GPU-hours. Do not proceed without a specific, named failure
   that the prompt cannot fix.
2. **Supervised fine-tuning** on gate-outcome labels. Most teams should stop
   here.
3. **Reinforcement learning**, only if you can trust your reward. RL's
   ceiling is set by the reward function, which is set by the judge — and a
   judge's verdicts are usable as a reward only after calibration against
   human decisions shows Cohen's kappa of at least 0.78. Agreement above 0.82
   is treated as suspicious rather than reassuring — improbably high
   agreement with humans usually means leakage, not brilliance. The kappa
   thresholds in the docs and the code are kept in sync by a CI test.

One more constraint ties training back to retrieval: RL rollouts and
production inference call the same SQL retrieval functions through the same
tool surface, enforced byte-identical by a parity test. A policy trained
against even a slightly different retrieval environment learns to exploit
the difference.

## See it yourself

The export path runs locally against the decisions you made in
[your first merge](/first-merge):

```bash
uv run fde-training export-sft --stats
```

With a fresh database the counts are small or zero — the point is that the
pipeline from review decision to training example is real, running code, not
a diagram.
